Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Ilam** (encircling the historical domains of **دره‌شهر**), the legendary lore of **Inshushinak ‌** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Inshushinak ‌** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour Inshushinak, perhaps literally meaning “Lord of Susa” was one of the major gods of the Elamites and the protector deity of Susa (An ancient city located in southwest Iran).He played an important role as a god connected to royal power in the official ideology of many Elamite dynasties. King Atta-Hushu of the Sukkalmah dynasty called himself "the shepherd of the god Inshushinak."He was also a divine witness of contracts, similar to Mesopotamian ShamashInshushinak was closely related to the afterlife and appeared as a judge of the dead in the so-called Susa funerary texts.The Elamite Zigurat Chogha Zanbil in Khuzestan Province in Iran, built about 1250 BC by the king Untash-Napirisha was mainly to honor Inshushinak.Some scholars believe that during the Achaemenid period of the Persian Empire, starting 7th century BCE, Inshushinak began losing prevalence among southwestern Iranians."

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Inshushinak ‌** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Ilam to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Inshushinak ‌** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
