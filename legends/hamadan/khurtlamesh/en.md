Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hamadan** (encircling the historical domains of **همدان / الوند**), the legendary lore of **Khurtlamesh** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Khurtlamesh** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami In Hamedan, the resurrected dead are called "Khortlamesh". This Turkish word means "resurrected dead" or "awakened". The people of this area believe that the devil likes to get into people's bodies. Whenever dead bodies came out of their graves, people said that the corpses were possessed by demons. In the old days, when people had a stroke and suddenly fainted, others buried them with the assumption that they were dead, and when the person regained consciousness, they could get out of the shallow graves.‌ …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Khurtlamesh** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hamadan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Khurtlamesh** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
