Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Gilan** (encircling the historical domains of **رشت / لاهیجان**), the legendary lore of **Ash & Fatemeh** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Ash & Fatemeh** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz 'Ash' is a mythical creature in the belief of people in Alika (A village in Mazandaran province, located in northern Iran). It is a huge, appalling creature with hair all over its body and it lives in Alborz mountain and the northern part of Garmsar (Northern part of Iran). In the Alikaii dialect, 'Ash' means 'bear'. The reason why they named it 'Ash' is because of the bear's huge body and its scariness. There's also a story among people in Alika.The story is about a family in the countryside of Garmsar that had a beautiful daughter named 'Fatemeh'. When Fatemeh was born, her family put a 'kahoo mirkaa' necklace around her neck (A voodoo used by Tabari people). Years passed and Fatemeh turned into a grown woman. Ash, despite his evil nature, fell in love with her and decided to kidnap her. One day when Ash had a chance, he kidnapped Fatemeh and took her to his dark cave. He locked her up and forced her to live there with him. Years passed and Fatemeh gave birth to two kids that were half-human. One day when Fatemeh's father took his sheep to graze near Ash's cave, one of the goats entered the cave. When Fatemeh saw the goat she gave it food and sent it back. Days passed and the goat got used to the treatment. After Fatemeh was sure that the goat would come every day, she put her kahoo mirkaa necklace around its neck to inform her family of her whereabouts. When the goat went back to get its milk, Fatemeh's family noticed the necklace and were shocked. They followed the goat the next day and entered the cave to find their daughter. When they saw her, she was making dough. After seeing her family she left the dough and went home with them.When Ash returned and realized what had happened, he spent days near the tent where Fatemeh and her family lived and without showing himself, sang 'Yāl vang vang, khamir torsh torsh, Fatemeh Hō, biu' that translates to 'the kids are crying, the dough has turned sour, Fatemeh, come back'. When he realized that Fatemeh was not going to pay attention to him, he cut his kids in half and threw the part of their faces that were human in front of their tent. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Ash & Fatemeh** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Gilan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Ash & Fatemeh** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
