Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Gilan** (encircling the historical domains of **رشت / لاهیجان**), the legendary lore of **‏Drvaspa** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **‏Drvaspa** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour Drvaspa Is the name of a Zoroastrian divinity typically assumed as a goddess comparable to Celtic Epona. She is associated with horses and a protectorate of four legged animals on earth. Ancient Iranians celebrated the fourteenth day of the last month of winter in her honor, and would not cook or eat meat. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **‏Drvaspa** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Gilan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **‏Drvaspa** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
