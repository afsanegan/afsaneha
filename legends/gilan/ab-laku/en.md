Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Gilan** (encircling the historical domains of **رشت / لاهیجان**), the legendary lore of **Ab Laku** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Ab Laku** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Āb-Laku in the Gilaki dialect means turtle (dialect used in Gilan, located in the northern part of Iran), but also sounds like [the water girl]. There is a folk tale around this word among the Gilak. It’s said that a long time ago, there used to be a woman that had just gotten married. One day when she was alone in the house, she decided to warm up some water and pour it into a basin to wash up. Suddenly, while she was showering, her father-in-law came home (old houses in northern parts of Iran, such as Gilan and Mazandaran, didn't have fences). Not wanting to be seen naked by her father-in-law, the girl hid under the basin and turned into Āb-Laku/turtle. According to another story, one day, the newlywed and her mother-in-law got into a fight in the bath. The mother-in-law cursed at the girl. Feeling embarrassed, the girl put the bowl on her head and turned into Āb-Laku/turtle. According to another story, one day the newlywed and her mother-in-law got into a fight while, inside the bath. The mother-in-law cursed at the girl. Feeling embarrassed, the girl put the bowl on her head and turned into Āb-Laku/turtle. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Ab Laku** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Gilan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Ab Laku** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
