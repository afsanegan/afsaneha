Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Ardabil** (encircling the historical domains of **سبلان**), the legendary lore of **YEDDĪ BĀJĪ** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **YEDDĪ BĀJĪ** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Yalda Rasekhi According to the people of Givi (located in Ardabil province, northwestern Iran), there's a big stone on a mountain that has the shape of seven women with a child sitting on one of their shoulders. This stone is known as YEDDĪ BĀJĪ, which means the Seven Sisters. There's an interesting story behind it that says, in the past, when there were no roads, and people had to walk through the mountains and uneven passways, the Seven Sisters existed. One day when they were going home from bathing, they saw bandits from afar. After seeing them, they all prayed to God to turn them into stones, and their wish came true.Translated by Sara Mohebbi … Oral history, Local Lore"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **YEDDĪ BĀJĪ** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Ardabil to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **YEDDĪ BĀJĪ** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
