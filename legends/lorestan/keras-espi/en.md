Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Lorestan** (encircling the historical domains of **خرم‌آباد**), the legendary lore of **Keras espi** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Keras espi** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Yalda Rasekhi In the beliefs of the people living in the northern part of Lorestan (Lak people), there's a creature called [Keras Espi]. This creature is tall (around 3 meters) and wears a long white outfit. People describe him with hair and a long beard. Keras Espi usually stands next to the roads, and his preys are drivers who drive by themselves. He asks questions very loudly and expects them to answer. Those who have seen Keras Espi say that he knew what their name and their father's name were and expected them to stop the car and answer his three questions. Apparently, it's not clear what he asks about or what he does to the drivers after getting the answer. When people first see him from afar, he looks like a halo of light, but when he gets closer, his scary face becomes clear. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Keras espi** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Lorestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Keras espi** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
