Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Lorestan** (encircling the historical domains of **خرم‌آباد**), the legendary lore of **Dig be sar** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Dig be sar** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami In the tales of the people of Borujerd (western Iran), there is a character named [Dig Be Sar] which means [cauldron/pot on the head]. It was often described as a human figure with black hands whose victims were mostly children. Interestingly, these stories were told to lull children to sleep. Sometimes parents would put a pot on their heads to look like [Dig Be Sar]. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Dig be sar** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Lorestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Dig be sar** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
