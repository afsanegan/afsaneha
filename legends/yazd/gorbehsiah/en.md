Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Yazd** (encircling the historical domains of **یزد / میبد**), the legendary lore of **Gprbeh Siah** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Gprbeh Siah** within the mythological taxonomy of *Jin*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour In many parts of Iran, there is a belief that the black cat is a Jinn (Genie). It is said that the Jinn appearing as a black cat is of the malicious kind. For this reason, they believe that when a black cat enters a room at night, people should greet it. Bothering or harming a black cat could drive the person into insanity. If someone spills water on a black cat, they might get warts. Some people also thought that a human's Doppelgänger might appear in a black cat costume and that if you bothered the cat, you might hurt yourself. … …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Gprbeh Siah** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Yazd to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Gprbeh Siah** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
