Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Kermanshah** (encircling the historical domains of **کرمانشاه / بیستون**), the legendary lore of **Wolf of Gudin** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Wolf of Gudin** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Yalda Rasekhi Among people from Gudin village (western Iran), there's a story that's been passed through from generation to generation:A guy named Haj Tahmasb used to live in Gudin village. He had a son who went against his father and married a tribeswoman. After a while, the girl started going out every night and when his husband noticed, he told Haj Tahmasb that his wife was seeing another man. One night when the girl woke up to go out, his husband and Haj Tahmasb took a cudgel and ran after her to kill her, but when they reached the mountain, they were faced with a humanoid wolf (werewolf). The creature said in a dialect: 'meh gorg nesam gorgasam, aroose haj tahmasam' which translates to 'I'm not a wolf, I'm a werewolf. I'm Haj Tahmasb's daughter-in-law'.They got scared and ran away and Haj Tahmasb's daughter-in-law never returned home. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Wolf of Gudin** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Kermanshah to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Wolf of Gudin** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
