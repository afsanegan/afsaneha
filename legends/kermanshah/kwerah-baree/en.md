Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Kermanshah** (encircling the historical domains of **کرمانشاه / بیستون**), the legendary lore of **KAWERAH BAREE** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **KAWERAH BAREE** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz Kawera Bari is a mythical creature common among the people of Kermanshah, Ilam and Kurdistan, in Iran, known to be a monster with goat feet and a human torso. It’s been said that on the first night of autumn, these creatures stop by all the houses of the town to pick up some food for their trip, on their way of migration to warmer places. On this night, people would make a whole pot of a specific kind of soup, the Dane Koolane soup, and leave it in the kitchen all night for Kawera Bari to come and eat on its way. No one was allowed to eat any of this soup before the next morning. It’s believed that if the people follow this tradition, the Kawera Bari would send them it’s blessings and that family’s food supplies will be provided for all the winter. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **KAWERAH BAREE** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Kermanshah to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **KAWERAH BAREE** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
