Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Kermanshah** (encircling the historical domains of **کرمانشاه (بیستون و زاگرس کهن)**), the legendary lore of **Yek Sar Do Zanu** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Yek Sar Do Zanu** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfann Aghvami In northeastern parts of Iran, there are children's stories about a creature known as 'Yek Sar Do Zanu',. Its name translates to 'one head and two knees'. The reason why it's called 'Yek Sar Do Zanu' is that all parts of this creature's body are hidden, except for its head and knees. It is said that this creature scares children who are mischievous and nag a lot, and if they start whining, it will come and take them. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Yek Sar Do Zanu** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Kermanshah to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Yek Sar Do Zanu** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
