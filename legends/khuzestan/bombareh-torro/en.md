Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Khuzestan** (encircling the historical domains of **شوش / شوشتر**), the legendary lore of **BOMBAREH TORRO** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **BOMBAREH TORRO** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz In the belief of the people of Dezful and Shushtar (Southwestern Iran), there is a Jinn named "Bombare Turoo". He goes to people’s houses at dusk or dawn when the weather is dark, pretends to be a friend or family, and convinces the owner of the house to accompany him. But in the middle of the way, this creature puts the person on its shoulders and rapidly begins to grow begins to grow. It grows so tall that its head reaches the sky.The creature only shows weakness against two things:Metal and salt. So the person would have to show if they have any metal object or a salt rock to get Bombare Turoo to flee, if not, they should loudly yell, "Sizan Jul Duz Ara Nemek" meaning "You want the needle or the salt". By uttering this sentence, a person can save themselves from the tricks of the Jinn where it suddenly starts to deflate and become smaller. Some believe this creature resides in caves around the river "Dez" by a graveyard in the city of Dezfool, southwestern Iran. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **BOMBAREH TORRO** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Khuzestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **BOMBAREH TORRO** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
