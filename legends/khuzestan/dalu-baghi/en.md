Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Khuzestan** (encircling the historical domains of **شوش / شوشتر**), the legendary lore of **Dalu Baghi** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Dalu Baghi** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami According to Lurs of Khuzestan (Southwestern Iran), especially the people of Izeh (City in Khuzestan province), there is a creature called [Dalu-Baghi], which lives in wells. Dalu-Baghi grabs people who walk past the well and pulls them inside. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Dalu Baghi** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Khuzestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Dalu Baghi** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
