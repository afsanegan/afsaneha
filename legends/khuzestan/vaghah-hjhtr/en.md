Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Khuzestan** (encircling the historical domains of **شوش / شوشتر**), the legendary lore of **VAGHAH** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **VAGHAH** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami It is a story from among the people of southern Iran, especially Dezful The Waqah is the type of "Scops owl" that flies at night. The story is such: If a child cries a lot after birth, for routine days, it is usually said that he has been bit by "waqah". In another tradition, hearing the sound of a waqah at night indicates the possibility of the death of a young child, which can be prevented by saying the words "knife" or "salt". It is believed generally in many folklore in Iran that evil fewls metal objects of being exposed to salt. Some people splash water in the air when they hear the noise. The people of these regions believe that if a cluster of Espand (Wild Rue), a sheet of steel, a silk thread, and goat hair are placed in a baby's cradle, the baby will be protected from the evil eye. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **VAGHAH** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Khuzestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **VAGHAH** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
