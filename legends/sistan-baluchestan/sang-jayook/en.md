Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Sistan and Baluchestan** (encircling the historical domains of **سیستان (خاستگاه پهلوانان شاهنامه)**), the legendary lore of **SANG JAYOOK** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **SANG JAYOOK** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mohammad Rasoulipour In the beliefs of southeastern Iranians, specifically, the Baluch people, is the name of a jinni that torments its victims by throwing stones at them. The victim looks around to see the culprit but can find no one. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **SANG JAYOOK** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Sistan and Baluchestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **SANG JAYOOK** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
