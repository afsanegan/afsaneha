Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Sistan and Baluchestan** (encircling the historical domains of **هامون / زابل**), the legendary lore of **Hulu** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Hulu** within the mythological taxonomy of *Jin*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Among the beliefs of people from Baluchestan (a province in the southeast part of Iran), there is a Jin (in Muslim beliefs, it is the same as a genie) called Hulu which can disguise itself as a bull at night and attack people. According to them, jins get more disturbing and cause more trouble for the people on Sundays and Tuesdays.‌ Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Hulu** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Sistan and Baluchestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Hulu** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
