Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **PARI DARYĀYEE** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **PARI DARYĀYEE** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami In the belief of the people of Hormozgan (southern Iran), there is a legendary creature called a "Peri Daryayee", which is literally translated as "Sea Fairy" or often similar to what is known as a Mermaid in the West. A mermaid is an aquatic creature that is half human and half fish. It is said that she stands by the seashore, brushes her long hair with one hand, and holds a mirror in the other hand. In many stories, Peri Daryayees sometimes make predictions by compulsion. They give their supernatural powers to humans, and sometimes they fall in love with humans, or by seducing mortal humans who have fallen in love with them, they sink them to the bottom. They do not cause any harm to humans and are considered harmless phantom creatures. In another story, mermaids fall in love with skilled sailors, and when the weather becomes stormy and the sailors sail on the sea with their boats, the mermaids like brave and successful sailors and stick their heads out of the water and try to steal them. There are legends of mermaids in the south of Iran near the Persian Gulf, and in Persian fictional texts, water people who live in the sea are also mentioned.The tale of the Sea Fairies is long known in Iran, in the book "Nuzhat al-Qulob", Hamdullah Mostofi, a thirteenth-century writer, writes about them in nearly identical terms to what is believed in southern Iran today. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **PARI DARYĀYEE** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **PARI DARYĀYEE** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
