Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **Madar Darya** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Madar Darya** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz In the belief of the people of the Persian Gulf, there is a creature called the "Mother of the Sea". The mother of the sea has an upper body similar to a human and a lower body similar to a fish. Belief in him is more prevalent among pearl fishermen. Some of them claim to have seen a woman underwater holding an infant. This woman asked the divers to make a cradle for her child and take it to her so that she would reward the fishermen with some large pearls in exchange for the cradle.Another diver says that when fishing for pearls in the deep sea, his eyes fell on a woman who was rocking her baby's cradle. The woman asks the diver to stay with her and her child. The diver points and says that he has no more breath to stay, but the woman creates a bubble around him with her breath and tells him to stay with them for a while. Suddenly, some mermaids came with colorful baskets to celebrate the baby's birthday and for good health. The people on the ship, who were worried about the diver being late, pulled the rope, one end of which was tied to the diver's leg, and brought the unconscious diver to the deck. After regaining consciousness, he tells the story of his mother and the cradle.Pearl fishermen believe if the mother of the sea talks to a diver, their catch will prosper. Similarly, if a cradle gets caught in the fishermen's net, they return the cradle to the sea and quickly move away from the place in fear of retaliation. Mermaid"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Madar Darya** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Madar Darya** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
