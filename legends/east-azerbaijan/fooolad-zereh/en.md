Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **FULAD ZEREH** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **FULAD ZEREH** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mohammad Rasoulipour "Foladzare" literally meaning "Steel Armored" is the name of the antagonist in the story of the famous literary hero "Amir Arslan". He is a towering horned Deev with magical powers including the ability to fly. It is said that he'd glide the air, capture beautiful women, and try to seduce them. At first, he was the commander of the army of the king of the fairies (Malek Khazan) who ruled over the land of "Zahrgiah". But after the death of King Khazan, Foladzare, who was fascinated with the queen, took over the throne by turning the king's successors and companions into stone in their castle. Also, the mother of "Foladzare", who was a mighty witch, had made "Foladzare" invulnerable to weapons with her magic, except for a sword called "Emerald Engraver's Sword" which belonged to Solomon the Prophet. This sword, which was the only way to destroy this monster, was kept and carefully guarded by "Foladzare". The famous Amir Arslan, who was destined to destroy "Foladzare", went to the land of magic to free his lover, "Farokh Laqa", and "Foladzare", who knew this, tried to find the hero and kill him. In the end, after the fight between these two, Amir Arslan defeated "Foladzare" and his mother with the emerald sword and then burned their bodies, mixed their ashes with water, and then applied the paste to all those who had been turned into stone, restoring them to life." …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **FULAD ZEREH** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **FULAD ZEREH** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
