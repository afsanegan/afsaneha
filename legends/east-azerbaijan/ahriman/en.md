Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **Angra Mainyu** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Angra Mainyu** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour Angra Mainyu, also called Ahriman, is considered God’s adversary in the Ancient Iranian religion, Zoroastrianism. Described as Div of Divs, chief among demons, is the destructor of the creation of Ahura Mazda (The High Lord). In early Avestan texts, Angra Mainyu has less of a worldly manifestation and is more of a cosmic concept. Though in later texts and art of Zoroastrians it is imagined as “borrowing” worldly manifestations of a man, lizard, bird, or Azhudahak (Demonic Serpent/Dragon). …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Angra Mainyu** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Angra Mainyu** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
