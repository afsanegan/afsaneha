Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **Bushasp** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Bushasp** within the mythological taxonomy of *Div*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mojde Daneshvar [Bushasp] or [Bushiast], is a female Div that causes drowsiness and laziness in people. The pleasure behind sleeping early in the morning comes from her. She's usually mentioned as [Dareghō gava], which means [the one with long hands].In the 18th Fargard (each section of the Avesta is called a Fargard) of Vendidad (a collection of texts within the greater compendium of the Avesta) is said that 'when in the order of Sroasha (Angel of Conscience), the rooster started singing, "wake up human. Wake up and start praying. Curse the demons or Bushasp will come and put the world to sleep saying, 'sleep. It's not the time for you to open your eyes yet. You shouldn't care about the Good Thoughts, Good Words, and Good Deeds." In the Iranian culture, Bushasp means 'dream' and dreaming. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Bushasp** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Bushasp** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
