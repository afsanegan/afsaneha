Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **East Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **تبریز**), the legendary lore of **Khar-e-Dajjal** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Khar-e-Dajjal** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz [Khar-e-Dajjal] is Dajjal's (an evil figure in Islamic studies. Similar to an antichrist) famous donkey, which appears at the beginning of judgment day. This donkey gets everybody from everywhere they are to join it by deceiving them. Its arrival is one of the signs of the beginning of judgment day. There are different narrations about this donkey in the beliefs of the Iranian people. Apparently, this donkey is big. Each step it takes is about six parasangs (about four miles), and the sound of an instrument comes from each hair on its body. Bread falls out of its ears, and its dung is of figs and dates. When the donkey arrives, starveling people follow it so they can eat and enjoy the music, but the donkey deceives them and takes them to purgatory or a wasteland which they can't come back from safely. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Khar-e-Dajjal** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of East Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Khar-e-Dajjal** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
