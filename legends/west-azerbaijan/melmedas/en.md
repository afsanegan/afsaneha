Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **West Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **ارومیه**), the legendary lore of **Melmedas** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Melmedas** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz In Iranian beliefs, there is a woman named Melmedas or Om Eldas(Arabic). Melmadas is a very beautiful woman who adorns herself with precious gems. She appears to the youth and seduces them. Whenever a young man is deceived by Melmedas, she cuts the lustful young man with her sickle-shaped legs.The people of Hormuz Island believe that she resides next to the salt valleys, by a big konar tree (Persian ironwood tree). Under this tree, a beautiful woman sits around sunset, whose name is Melmadas. She combs her long hair and the men who see her fall in love with her charming look. When men start talking to her, Melmedas approaches them and suddenly changes face.Old-looking woman with legs like sickles. With his sickle-shaped hands, he cuts the deceived men to pieces.There is another story that Melmedas has a package of jewels with her. If a man is aware they are facing Melmedas, they can quickly take a handful of dirt or sand and sprinkle it in the eyes of Melmedas, take her jewelry, and run away. If he is deceived by Melmedas, he will end up being her victim.Melmedas can be considered similar to "Jehi" in Zoroastrian mythology and "Siren" in Greek mythology. The tale of the Sea Fairies is long known in Iran, in the book "Nuzhat al-Qulob", Hamdullah Mostofi, a thirteenth-century writer, writes about them in nearly identical terms to what is believed in southern Iran today."

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Melmedas** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of West Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Melmedas** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
