Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **West Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **ارومیه**), the legendary lore of **DIDOMAK** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **DIDOMAK** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz Didomak is a mythical creature known in Korukhak, in the South of Iran as a cursed bird. It was believed hearing its sound during the night, would bring bad luck. So, pouring water on the ground was a way to stop any bad luck. It is also believed that hearing Didomak during the night could mean that a baby is born. Some even believe that if they hear this bird over their house this means a traveler or a family member is safe and healthy. Whoever from the family hears the sound, they would say “good news” out loud. The name of this bird comes from its sound which is Mostly heard “dee do mak.” …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **DIDOMAK** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of West Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **DIDOMAK** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
