Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **West Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **ارومیه**), the legendary lore of **Hajebukhare** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Hajebukhare** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz Hajebukhare, in the belief of the people from Orumiyeh, is a Jinn that looks like black smoke and lives inside the stovepipes. By chanting 'Hajebukhare Chah Yukhareh', they ask him to come out and possess children. After coming out of the stovepipes, it drags the person it's looking for back inside the pipes and takes them away to unknown worlds and leaves them there. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Hajebukhare** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of West Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Hajebukhare** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
