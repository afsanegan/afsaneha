Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **West Azerbaijan** (encircling the historical domains of **ارومیه**), the legendary lore of **Daghlar Gulu** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Daghlar Gulu** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour There's a myth in Maku, a city in the West Azerbaijan province in Iran, about a creature named Daghlar-Gulu or Dayhlar-Guli that speaks like a human and defends nature. This humanoid wolf wanders in the mountains, wearing a gold and green outfit. Whenever Daghlar-Gulu sees a hunter, a groundsman, or anyone harming nature, it eats them. It normally starts with eating their tongue so that they won't scream. It then starts eating other parts of their body in front of them and hangs their corpse from the roof of their father's house. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Daghlar Gulu** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of West Azerbaijan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Daghlar Gulu** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
