Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Isfahan** (encircling the historical domains of **اصفهان / کاشان**), the legendary lore of **KUKU** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **KUKU** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Khale Kuku, or Khale Zarā, is a story about a kind of bird among the people of Isfahan that knows about the future. In this belief, Aunt Kuku has two types of songs. The first is its humming sound, which is considered to tell that something good is about to happen. If this bird sits on someone's house and makes the sound of a spinning wheel, it means that someone in that household is about to die soon. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **KUKU** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Isfahan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **KUKU** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
