Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Tehran** (encircling the historical domains of **ری / تهران**), the legendary lore of **Naam Gir** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Naam Gir** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz 'Nām-Gir', according to Afghan people's belief, is a creature similar to a Jinn. This creature can possess a human body. It calls the victim's name three times, and if the person answers all three times with 'yes', Nām-Gir will possess them. Whenever Nām-Gir possesses people, it steals their names and lives inside their bodies as them. The possessed people lose all control over their bodies, and Nām-Gir does horrible things while living inside them. Some people believe that if someone says 'yes' out loud for no reason, Nām-Gir has called them. If this happens people cover their mouths so they won't say 'yes' three times. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Naam Gir** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Tehran to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Naam Gir** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
