Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Tehran** (encircling the historical domains of **ری / تهران**), the legendary lore of **‏Gotu Karsanli** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **‏Gotu Karsanli** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Among Gezelbash Turks of Ramian (northern part of Iran), there was a tale of a woman with huge buttocks, carrying a [Bogh-che] (bindle or stuff wrapped in a cloth) who was called [Kársánle-göt]. She would take nosy kids who wandered in the woods alone and put them in her Bogh-Che.In Turkish, 'Göt' means buttocks, and 'Kársánle' is a big plate made of oak wood that they rolled the dough in. Sometimes this name is used as a hint to people who become overweight due to sitting behind desks for long hours and not walking enough. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **‏Gotu Karsanli** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Tehran to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **‏Gotu Karsanli** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
