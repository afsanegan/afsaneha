Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Tehran** (encircling the historical domains of **ری / تهران**), the legendary lore of **Shamhoroush** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Shamhoroush** within the mythological taxonomy of *Jin*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfann Aghvami Malek-Shamhoroush or Shamharoush (also known as Samhorous or Samharous) is one of the seven genie kings. Some also know him as Abā-alvalid (the child's father) or Abā-altteyr (the father of the bird). It's believed that he's associated with the planet Jupiter, the day Thursday, the Color Purple, and the metal Tin. According to believers of Jinns (genies), he's the head of the judiciary of genies. They say that he's so mighty and prestigious that no other genie, not even Iblis (leader of the devils in Islam), can defy his commands. Shamharoush, like other kings of genies, is a part of Arabian mythology. Iranian people who follow occult sciences also believe in his existence. Some say that he became a Muslim at the beginning of the Islamic era and was the leader of the Muslim Jinns. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Shamhoroush** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Tehran to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Shamhoroush** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
