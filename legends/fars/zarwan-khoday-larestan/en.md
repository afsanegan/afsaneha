In the sun-scorched, arid mountain plateaus of Larestan in southern Fars province, lies the ancient village of *Zarvan*. The settlement's name and oral traditions preserve an extraordinary linguistic and mythological survival of pre-Islamic Iranian antiquity: the veneration of *Zurvan*, the primordial divinity of Boundless Time and Cosmic Destiny. Documented in regional folklore studies of the Achomi-speaking population, the legend of *Zarwan Khoday* illustrates the profound metaphysical relationship between cosmic time and terrestrial ecology.

According to village elders, an ancient perennial spring once bubbled from a deep cavern at the foot of the mountain, governed directly by the cosmic cycles of the Lord of Time (*Zarwan Khoday*). Local lore depicted this deity as a majestic, silver-bearded sage cloaked in starlight seated upon the mountain's highest pinnacle, holding two golden hourglasses. The flowing sands regulated the changing seasons, the lifespans of mortals, and the underground hydrology of the valley. So long as the villagers lived in truthfulness and communal harmony, the celestial sands fell rhythmically, and the aquifer nourished their precious date palm groves.

> As the Achomi elders of Larestan observe: "The Lord of Time sits above the peak counting the grains of fate; when the designated hour strikes, neither deceit nor tyranny can endure, and the turn of destiny restores justice."

When corruption and greed gradually took root in the valley—as feudal overseers diverted the communal irrigation channels away from destitute orphans—Zarwan Khoday tilted the cosmic hourglass, halting the flow of sand. Instantly, the mountain spring vanished into the limestone abyss, scorching winds withered the date palms, and catastrophic famine threatened the settlement. Recognizing the spiritual origin of their calamity, the elders confessed their transgressions, restored the stolen water rights, and reconciled their disputes under the open sky. In response to their renewed virtue, the sands of destiny began to flow once more, and life-giving water surged through the subterranean channels. The myth endures in southern Iran as a solemn reminder of the moral accountability woven into the fabric of time.

### Anthropological & Folklore Analysis
The myth represents an exceptional survival of Zurvanite theology within the oral folklore of southern Iran. Key mythological motifs include the anthropomorphization of Time as an ecological regulator, the direct link between moral integrity and hydrological vitality, and the preservation of archaic mythological toponyms within Achomi (Larestani) linguistic culture.
