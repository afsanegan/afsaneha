Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **Khedr** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Khedr** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Zeinab Eslami Khedr, or Khedr the Prophet, is the name of a supernatural figure in the beliefs of many Iranians and neighboring countries. People said Khedr would appear to those who clean the doorsteps of a place of worship for forty days without being seen. In one story, Khedr appeared in front of a righteous man with financial problems. His family’s grain stash was finishing quickly, and he didn't know how to restock it, so he prayed to the lord. Khedr then appeared in front of him and gave him a hand full of grain. He said, 'put this with the rest of your supply, and it shall never end. But remember not to talk about this to anyone.' The man did as such and saw that his grain did not run out. Later he talked to a friend about what had happened and broke the promise. Once he returned home, he found that all the grains were gone. Translation: Mo.Rasoulipour"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Khedr** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Khedr** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
