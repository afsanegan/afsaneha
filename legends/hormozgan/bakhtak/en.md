Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **Bakhtak ‌** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Bakhtak ‌** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustrated by Mohammad Rasoulipour Bakhtak Is a name given to a supernatural creature, typically associated with sleep paralysis, referred to in the West as Night Hag. “It is a phenomenon during which a person feels the presence of a supernatural malevolent being which immobilizes the person as if sitting on their chest or the foot of their bed.” The visual descriptions differ from culture to culture or even from person to person who claims to have seen this creature. But commonly seen as a small, heavy being with sharp teeth, skin of tar, and a nose of clay. Compare with ‘Night Hag’ Bakhtakbymohammad.mahdi.amouzgaronSketchfab"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Bakhtak ‌** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Bakhtak ‌** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
