Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **Musa-Ku-Taghi ‌ ‌** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Musa-Ku-Taghi ‌ ‌** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz The legend of 'Musa Ku Taghi' is heard among the people of Khorasan (located in the eastern part of Iran). The story is about three brothers named Musa, Taghi, and Karim. One day they decided to go outside to play without asking their parents for permission. Taghi goes missing. Musa and Karim prayed to God to turn one of them into a bird to fly up in the skies and look for their brother, while the other one returned home and explained the situation to their parents. God answered their prayers and turned Karim into a bird. He flew up but couldn't find their brother, so he kept asking Musa about Taghi, saying "Musa Ku Taghi" (which means, 'Musa, where is Taghi?'). The sound of him asking is now recognized as tweeting. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Musa-Ku-Taghi ‌ ‌** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Musa-Ku-Taghi ‌ ‌** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
