Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **Mehrizad** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Mehrizad** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour After the Emperor of Darkness defeated Hormazbagh and his children and they fell to the Land of Darkness, the Father of Greatness tried to rescue them and bring them back to the Land of Light. The creation of the second group of deities happened at that time. Among those deities, 'Mehrizad' was created. To rescue Hormazbagh, Mehrizad went to the borders of the Land of Darkness and stretched his right hand for Hormazbagh to take. Hormazbagh took his hand and managed to escape the Land of Darkness. 'Amhar-sepandān' was tempered with the darkness, and they couldn't escape the land. Mehrizad and his five children went to the Land of Darkness one more time to save Amhar-sepandān. They had to create a materialistic world. The reason behind it was so that everything that was supposed to happen inside that world, and the movement of time, caused the darkness and the light to be separated. Mehrizad and the Mother of Life planned the creation of the materialistic world, and in a few steps, they created the world. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Mehrizad** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Mehrizad** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
