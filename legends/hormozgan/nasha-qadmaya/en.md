Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **Nasha Qadmaya** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Nasha Qadmaya** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour In Manichaean mythology, when the devil left his land to conquer the Land of Light, the Father of Greatness, the king of the Land of Light, created a goddess called 'the Mother of Life' to protect his land. The Mother of Light created 'Hormazbagh' (Nasha Qadmaya). Hormazbagh is the incarnation of the first heavenly human and the first worrier of the land of light. He had five children who were named 'Amhar-sepandān'.The devil defeated Amhar-sepandān in the battle, and Hormazbagh had to let go of his children. He went unconscious and fell deep into hell.When he woke up, he asked for the mother of life's aid, and to aid him, the mother of life asked for the father of greatness' help. Father of Greatness created the second group of deities and sent them to save Hormazbagh from hell. In the end, Hormazbagh was saved from hell. He ascended to the Land of Light next to the Mother of Light. Hormazbagh being saved from hell is the everlasting example of human souls being rescued from hell. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Nasha Qadmaya** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Nasha Qadmaya** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
