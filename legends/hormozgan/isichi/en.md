Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **ISICHI** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **ISICHI** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz In the belief of the people of Karsegan village in Najafabad, Isfahan province, there is a dwarf creature named "Isichi". He has a muddy nose and falls on people who are sleeping. The only way to escape from him is to grab his nose, which causes Isichi to run away. Among the signs of its presence, I can mention heaviness in the body, shortness of breath, and speech impediment during sleep.Some other regions consider Isichi similar to "Aal" and believe that Isichi steals children, takes them with him, and replaces them with his evil children.In another story, Aal (the enemy of new-born women), um-alSabiyan (the female demon who kidnaps babies and children), and Bakhtak are sometimes combined, and a creature is born that has all three characteristics and is called Isichi.The people of the Lanjanat region consider Isichi as a "Bakhtak" that attacks people at night and while they are sleeping, and they intend to suffocate the victim. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **ISICHI** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **ISICHI** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
