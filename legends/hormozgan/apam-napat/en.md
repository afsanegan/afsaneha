Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Hormozgan** (encircling the historical domains of **قشم / بندرعباس**), the legendary lore of **APĄM NAPĀT** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **APĄM NAPĀT** within the mythological taxonomy of *Deity*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour APĄM NAPĀT, meaning (Son of the Waters) is an Indo-Iranian divinity that, at a time, in ancient Iranian beliefs, was credited with the creation of Humans. In the Avesta, he is described as: “kingly, shining, Son of the Waters, who has swift horses, the hero who gives help when called upon. (It is) he who created men, he who shaped men, the god amid the waters, who is prayed to is swiftest of all to hear.” In Bundaheshn,“Every third year, many from non-Iranian lands gather together upon the summit of Mount Harborz (Alborz) in order to go into Iranian lands to cause harm and bring destruction to the world. Then the yazad Borz [APĄM NAPĀT] comes up from the depths of the water Arang and arouses, on the highest point of all that high mountain, the bird Čamrūš, which picks up all those from non-Iranian lands as a bird pecks up grain (Bundahišn 24.24).” (!) In the Vedas he is described as such:“Golden in form is he, like gold to look on, his color is like gold, the Son of Waters… his food is sacred oil of golden color.”"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **APĄM NAPĀT** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Hormozgan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **APĄM NAPĀT** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
