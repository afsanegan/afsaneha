Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Mazandaran** (encircling the historical domains of **آمل / دماوند**), the legendary lore of **BOZDIVAN** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **BOZDIVAN** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami "Bozdivan" (literally, Goat Deevs) is a type of demon that has been kept alive orally in some parts of the northern plateau of Iran and other parts of Iran in local beliefs. It is said that these demons roam in damp places, such as rivers, forests and reservoirs. It is even said that during drought and extreme cold, they may approach the settlements to provide food and water. It is believed that these creatures are not harmful to humans unless they feel threatened. This type of deevs are reportedly afraid of metal objects. For this reason, some people always carry a pin with them to bring water from the river or the cold house. Some people believe that Bozdivan and Gavdivan (Cow Deevs) helped Fereydoun Pishdadi entrap Zahhak the serpent king. There is mentioned two famous bozdeevs, one was Ghash-ash, the son of Faghtash, in the army of white deevs of Amol (Region in northern iran) , and the other was Zahnash, the son of Qaanash, one of the commanders of the Jinn army of Sultan Muhammad Khwarazmshah (13th century King of Iran). In the belief of the people of Mazandaran (Northern Iran), Boz Deevs lives in a tribal manner and have a matriarchal community. They also believe that the wisest of the tribe turn into trees after death. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **BOZDIVAN** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Mazandaran to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **BOZDIVAN** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
