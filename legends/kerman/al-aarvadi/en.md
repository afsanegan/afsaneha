Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Kerman** (encircling the historical domains of **کرمان / بم**), the legendary lore of **Al-Aarvadi** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Al-Aarvadi** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Farima Keshavarz Aal Arvadi, in the beliefs of the people of Azarbayjan (Northwestern Iran), is the name of a Jinn that lives in the mountains. People say this creature is dangerous to women who have just given birth. According to legends, Aal Arvadi enters village homes and stuffs the new mother's mouth with charcoal that her mouth is kept wide open. Then she reaches inside the body of the victim through her mouth, takes out the liver, and eats it. Some believe that this creature also takes the newborns. They say this creature has such long breasts that she can throw them over her shoulders. Other than toward new mothers and newborns, she is said to be usually passive toward humans, but if she catches someone in the mountains, she will take them to the well near her house and force them to dig more and make her well bigger. Translation: Mo.Rasoulipour"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Al-Aarvadi** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Kerman to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Al-Aarvadi** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
