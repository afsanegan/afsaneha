Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Kerman** (encircling the historical domains of **کرمان / بم**), the legendary lore of **‏Nahankal Div** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **‏Nahankal Div** within the mythological taxonomy of *Div*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Nahankal-Div is a beast in the Sām-nāma.‏The story is about Sām. Sām was in love with the daughter of the king of China. Her name was Pari-dokht. The king said that the only way he would give Sām his blessing was if he managed to put down a Div called Nahankal. Nahankal was the Div who kept causing problems for him and his kingdom.‏Accepting the condition, Sām goes to war against the Div. However, Nahankal was a clever Div. He had an army of beasts fighting for him. They fought, and Nahankal captured Sām and his companions. After barely saving his companions, Sām manages to capture the beast. Nahankal then deceives him by saying that he will promise to apologize to the king if Sām frees him and that he was only causing problems because he also was in love with Pari-Dokht but had no hope and chance of bonding with her. Hearing that, Sām spares his life and takes Nahankal with him. Nahankal, though, attacks him using his army and frees himself.‏In the final battle, when Nahankal was going to impale Sām on his horns, Sām broke them and put down the beast. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **‏Nahankal Div** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Kerman to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **‏Nahankal Div** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
