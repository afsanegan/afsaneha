Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Golestan** (encircling the historical domains of **گرگان**), the legendary lore of **Isagh-Misagh** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Isagh-Misagh** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami Isagh-Misagh is the name of two birds in the beliefs of Ghezelbash Turks in Ramyan city of Golestan province. They are imagined as owl-shaped with human heads. Isagh and Misagh were two brothers whose father was a milkman, and he short-changed people by adding water into the milk. Because of that, a curse turned his sons into owls. According to the Ramyan people, Isagh and Misagh get separated. To find each other, the two birds sing on top of the cypress trees on spring nights, but they never find each other. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Isagh-Misagh** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Golestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Isagh-Misagh** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
