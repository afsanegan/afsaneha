Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Golestan** (encircling the historical domains of **گرگان**), the legendary lore of **Nane-sho** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Nane-sho** within the mythological taxonomy of *Jin*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour In the beliefs of people in Gorgan, there was a woman called [Nane-Sho]. She was a tall and thin woman with long breasts. At night, she grabbed a sack and put it on her shoulders. she walked on the roof of houses with this sack, and only her red eyes were visible. She sometimes made herself look like children's mothers and deceived them. She then put them inside her sack and kidnapped them. People used her to keep children quiet at night.In southern Kerman (Southeastern Iran), there's a creature with the same name. Parents used it to scare kids from going out. In Torbat-e Heydariyeh (Northeastern Iran), also, there's an evil creature named [Madar-Sho] that people sang a song about to their kids to put them to sleep.They sang 'Madar-Sho, sine lekato, bokhor o bodo'. [Sine Lekato] means wrinkly chests. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Nane-sho** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Golestan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Nane-sho** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
