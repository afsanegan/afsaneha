Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Ghoolak** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Ghoolak** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Gholak|Ghulak Illustration by Farima Keshavarz Qulak/Ghulak In southern Iranian lore, Ghulak is believed to be a shape-shifting creature who laughs and demeans people. If someone goes out of the house at night or at dawn, they might see a large donkey who appears friendly at first. So much that it appears to insist on giving you a ride on its back. Once on the donkey, after traveling a certain distance, Ghulak would start speaking in a mocking and would laugh at its rider or throw the rider and put the load on them. Some believe that Ghulak appears as a giant with a human face and finds travelers at night. During the journey, it changes face and mocks them, calling their name. Tho it is not mentioned that this creature would directly harm humans, it would be a very scary sight and surprize that a donkey or a giant call your name and mock you. …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Ghoolak** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Ghoolak** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
