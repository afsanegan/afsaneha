Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Kaj kolah khan** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Kaj kolah khan** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Yalda Rasekhi The name 'Kaj Kolah Khan' (the guy with a tilted hat) still exists in the minds of Mashhad's old people.In the past, when someone was courting a girl, there was a tradition. According to this tradition, if the families of both sides were content with the marriage, they would break a sugar cane. The broken sugar cane allowed someone called 'Kaj Kolah khan' to come at night to steal the bride. Then, on the same night, the bride's father and brother would watch the house from the inside, and the groom's father and brother would watch the house from the outside to keep 'Kaj Kolah Khan' out and protect the bride. Also, the mother of the bride would tie her own leg to the bride's leg with a scarf.The next morning, after managing to keep Kaj Kolah Khan out, they all stayed for breakfast at the bride's house. They would also drink their tea with the broken sugar cane from the previous night. Dancing and partying are also a part of this ceremony.About putting on a tilted hat:People living in Khanqah used to wear their hats, tilted. The tilted hat was also used in the 'Sama' dance or in Hafez and Rumi's poems. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Kaj kolah khan** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Kaj kolah khan** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
