Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Shevahe** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Shevahe** within the mythological taxonomy of *موجود افسانه‌ای*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami You might hear the name 'Shevahe' among the people from Kurdistan province (Placed in the western part of Iran). It's the name of a creature that kills or takes newborns or children under the age of one. It would attack infants left alone in a room. If an infant died, they said that 'Shevahe' was the one who took the baby.Apparently, none of the Kurdish people have ever seen Shevahe, and there's no visual description for it, but they highly believe that 'Shevahe' is behind the death of infants who were not sick or were not killed by some accident. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Shevahe** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Shevahe** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
