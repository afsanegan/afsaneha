Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Shagrkirband** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Shagrkirband** within the mythological taxonomy of *Div*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Mo.Rasoulipour Šagrkirband - or šagrkirbandan (plural) are the names given a pair of lion-shaped demons in Manichean belief (Ancient Iranian Religion). These two demons were created by Az, the mother of all demons, in what is called The Third creation. Since the previous two creations failed to produce unpolluted humans, a third messenger was sent to wipe humans' seed of demons. Az, seeing this as a danger, created the Šagrkirbandan as a male and female to mate and produce offspring. This was done and the offspring were devoured by Az and the Šagrkirbandan and produced the first two humans in the third creation named, Gēhmurd and Murdiyānag. Translation: Mo.Rasoulipour …"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Shagrkirband** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Shagrkirband** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
