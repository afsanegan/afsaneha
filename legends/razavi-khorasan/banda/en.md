Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Banda** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Banda** within the mythological taxonomy of *Jin*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Zeinab Eslami According to people living in Hezar-Masjed (central Khorasan, located in northeastern Iran), there's an ugly and terrifying Jin called 'Banda'. Banda sits above people's heads when they're sleeping. If people hide under the covers, it would leave them alone but would strangle them to death. Banda always wears black and hide's its face. It lives inside the mountains and when the weather gets cold, it leaves the place. The reason behind telling this story was to keep the children warm and safe during cold winters. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Banda** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Banda** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
