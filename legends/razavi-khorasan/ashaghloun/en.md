Preserved across centuries within the rich oral folklore of **Razavi Khorasan** (encircling the historical domains of **نیشابور / توس**), the legendary lore of **Ashaghloun** stands as a compelling testament to Iran's enduring mythic imagination. Transmitted across hearths, mountain paths, and rural caravans, the tale has survived through the living memories of local elders.

Folklorists generally categorize **Ashaghloun** within the mythological taxonomy of *Div*. As with many traditional entities of Iranian folk belief, it embodies both the untamed natural elements and the ancestral anxieties regarding solitary wilderness. Classical collectors recorded its traditional traits as follows:

> "Illustration by Erfan Aghvami In 'Manichaean' mythology, there's a creature called 'Ashghaloun'. Ashghaloun and its twin, Pisous, were both 'Div-zad' (literally translates to 'demon-born'). The devil created them to devour the small rays of light that appeared in the Land of Darkness. They belonged to the Deities, who were one of Amhar-sepandān (children of the first man ever created, according to Manichaeism). The Deities were locked in the land of evil. Ashghaloun and Pisous ate the rays of light, and after mating with the Deities, they created two human beings named, Gahmard and Mardiyaneh. They had the rays of light inside them, which were devoured by the demons, parts of Āz, and other demons' anger and lust inside them. Translation: Sarah Mohebbi Moghadam"

In local oral tradition, an encounter with **Ashaghloun** was regarded as a formidable threshold between familiar domestic reality and the unseen realm. Elders customarily advised night travelers, shepherds, and caravan drivers traversing the historic passes of Razavi Khorasan to heed ancient apotropaic rituals—such as carrying iron amulets, maintaining solemn reverence towards nature, and never uttering provocative words—to pass through its territorial domain unharmed.

Today, the chronicle of **Ashaghloun** forms an invaluable component of Iran's intangible cultural heritage, offering modern readers an evocative window into how ancient communities interpreted their ecology, mystery, and moral balance.
